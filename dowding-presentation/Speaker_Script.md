# Speaker script: The battle won by information

About 21 minutes at 130 words per minute, plus Q&A. Speaker numbers are suggestions, so reassign them to your group.

## Slide 1: Front page

[Speaker 1, about 20 seconds]
Good morning. Our leadership quality is effective communication. Our story is about a battle that was decided as much by information as by aircraft, and about the man who built the network that carried that information: Air Chief Marshal Hugh Dowding.

## Slide 2: Britain stands alone

[Speaker 1, about 1 minute]
Imagine it is July 1940, and you command Britain's fighters. Germany has defeated France in six weeks, and on 22 June France signed an armistice. The German army stands on the French coast, and Britain is almost alone.
But Germany cannot simply sail across the Channel. Before any invasion, its air force must destroy the Royal Air Force. On 10 July, the attack begins. You can see the defenders here: Spitfires of No. 610 Squadron, photographed two weeks later.
Now, a question for you. German raids can come from anywhere along the coast. Where would you send these fighters?
Keep your answer in mind. It is exactly the problem Dowding faced.

## Slide 3: Part 1 divider

[Speaker 1, about 10 seconds]
Part 1, the story. One question runs through it: how did Britain meet every raid in time, with no fighters to spare?

## Slide 4: 750 British fighters, 2,250 German aircraft

[Speaker 1, about 50 seconds]
First, the enemy, and it was a strong one. The Luftwaffe had just helped defeat Poland, Norway and France.
Look at the chart. On 10 August 1940, Germany had more than 1,300 bombers and more than 950 fighters ready to fly. Britain had 1,100 fighters ready to fly, but trained pilots for only about 750 of them.
So fighter against fighter, the two sides were close. The problem was the bombers. Britain's 750 fighters had to find and stop more than 1,300 bombers, while German fighters protected them.
So every squadron had to be in the right place. A squadron sent to the wrong place could not reach the real raid in time.

## Slide 5: Twenty minutes to meet each raid

[Speaker 1, about 50 seconds]
And there was very little time.
A radar station in Kent could detect a raid forming over France about 20 minutes before it reached London. 11 Group, which defended London and the south-east, warned its controllers about exactly this.
Out of those 20 minutes, the network needed about 4 to put the raid on the operations room table. And at times the controller had about 2 minutes to decide.
So every minute spent passing a report along was a minute less for the fighters to reach the raid.

## Slide 6: Seeing the raid was not enough

[Speaker 2, about 1 minute]
Let's try a thought experiment. You are a radar operator. On your screen, 100 aircraft are crossing the Channel. What do you do?
Option A: you phone the nearest airfield. A few fighters take off on their own. Nobody else knows where the raid is going, and the other airfields sit and wait.
Option B: you report to headquarters. Each level studies the report and passes an order down. By the time the order reaches the pilots, the bombs have already fallen.
Both options fail. So seeing the raid was only the start. The hard part was getting the right report to the right person in time. Britain solved this problem with a network built for exactly this job. Who built it?

## Slide 7: The man who built the network

[Speaker 2, about 1 minute]
Hugh Dowding. In this photo from June 1940, he is the man in the centre with his hands behind his back, while the King decorates one of his pilots.
Dowding had one clear idea. In our words: send each squadron to a raid that someone has already reported, instead of sending it to search the sky.
In 1935, as the RAF's head of research, he backed radar after a successful test near Daventry. In 1936, he became the first head of Fighter Command. And over the next four years, he linked radar stations, observers, operations rooms and pilots into one network.
Let's follow a raid through that network, in four steps. Each step solves a problem left by the one before.

## Slide 8: Step 1: Report

[Speaker 2, about 1 minute]
Step 1: report.
On the left is a Chain Home radar station. About 20 of them stood along the coast. But radar had a weakness: the stations faced out to sea. Once the raid crossed the coast, radar could not track it.
So what did Dowding do? He used people. In the middle is the Observer Corps: about 30,000 volunteers at about 1,000 posts across the country. When a raid crossed the coast, they spotted it by eye and reported it by telephone.
But the radar plots had a problem of their own, shown at the bottom. Several plots often described the same raid, and some disagreed about where it was or how big it was. If every plot went straight to a commander, he would see far more raids than really existed.

## Slide 9: Step 2: Filter

[Speaker 2, about 1 minute]
Step 2: filter.
Radar reports went first to the Filter Room at Fighter Command headquarters. There, staff compared the plots, removed the duplicates and kept one track for each raid. Observer reports went to Observer Centres, which plotted them for their own area.
This is the step that Option B lacked. In Dowding's own account, each filtered track went by telephone to the Command, Group and Sector tables at the same time. Command, Group and Sector all saw the same raid in the same place at the same moment. Nobody waited for an order to be passed down.
But now three levels of commanders had to read the same table quickly, and they had to know how old each plot was.

## Slide 10: Step 3: Display

[Speaker 2, about 1 minute]
Step 3: display.
This photo shows the operations room at Fighter Command headquarters, with plotters around the map table and officers above.
The sector clock had red, yellow and blue segments, each 5 minutes long. Plotters used markers of the same colour, so a controller could see at once how old each plot was.
A raid block showed the raid's name and its estimated strength, and an arrow showed its direction. The tote board showed the state of each squadron, such as "at readiness" or "left ground".
A plotter at headquarters and a controller at a sector station read their own tables in the same way. One last problem remained: the pilot. A pilot in a cockpit needed his orders in a few words.

## Slide 11: Step 4: Order

[Speaker 3, about 1 minute]
Step 4: order.
Group decided which sector met each raid and with how many fighters. Then the sector controller spoke to the pilots by radio, in the code words Dowding lists in his despatch.
"Scramble" meant take off. "Vector 230" meant fly a course of 230 degrees. "Angels 20" meant fly at 20,000 feet. "Pancake" meant land. A whole order took only a few words.
The photo shows pilots of No. 19 Squadron running to their aircraft. It was staged for the camera, but a real scramble looked much like this.
So: report, filter, display, order. That is the path from a radar plot over France to a squadron climbing to meet the raid.

## Slide 12: We had nothing like it

[Speaker 3, about 45 seconds]
How well did it work? Let's ask the enemy.
Adolf Galland was a German fighter commander in 1940. He later wrote that the British had "an extraordinary advantage", which was "their radar and fighter control network and organisation". And then: "We had nothing like it."

## Slide 13: No reserves, so every order had to be right

[Speaker 4, about 1 minute]
15 September 1940. Churchill visits the No. 11 Group operations room at Uxbridge and watches the plotters move marker after marker across the table as German raids come in. He later wrote: "I now asked, 'What other reserves have we?' 'There are none,' said Air Vice Marshal Park."
Park had committed every squadron he had. With no reserves left, a squadron sent to the wrong place could not be replaced. Every order had to send a squadron to a real raid. This is the moment the network was built for.
Germany lost about 56 to 60 aircraft that day. Two days later, Hitler postponed the invasion of Britain until further notice.

## Slide 14: Right information, right person, in time

[Speaker 4, about 1.25 minutes]
So what is effective communication? Our definition: effective communication gets the right information to the right person in time, in a form that person can act on.
Each part of this definition comes from somewhere.
"The right information, to the right person" comes from Keyton, as cited in Lunenburg: communication is "transmitting information and common understanding from one person to another". In the network, one filtered track per raid reached every operations table.
"In a form that person can act on" comes from Spitzberg, who describes competence as an impression of effectiveness and appropriateness. "Vector 230, Angels 20" told a pilot where to fly, in a few words.
"In time" comes from the story itself. Neither scholar mentions time, but a raid could reach London about 20 minutes after radar first detected it.
So the two scholars agree that a message must reach the other person. They differ in focus: Keyton looks at what is transmitted, while Spitzberg looks at how the receiver judges it.

## Slide 15: Part 2 divider

[Speaker 4, about 10 seconds]
Part 2. Two theories help us answer one question: why did the network work?

## Slide 16: Theory 1: The 7 Cs

[Speaker 4, about 1 minute]
Our first theory is the 7 Cs of communication, from the business communication textbook by Murphy, Hildebrandt and Thomas. They list seven qualities of a good message. We have turned each one into a question a sender can ask.
Clarity: what is the one main point? Correctness: are the facts and tone right? Conciseness: can it be said in fewer words? Concreteness: is it backed by specific facts? Completeness: does the reader have all they need to act? Consideration: is it written from the reader's side? Courtesy: is it respectful, even when firm?
The 7 Cs work best on a single message from one sender. In Part 3 we test one of Dowding's radio orders against them.

## Slide 17: Theory 2: Shannon and Weaver

[Speaker 5, about 1.25 minutes]
Our second theory comes from engineering. In 1949, Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver described communication as a chain.
An information source creates a message. A transmitter turns it into a signal. The signal travels through a channel. A receiver turns it back into a message, and the message reaches its destination. On the way, a noise source can distort the signal.
Weaver added three levels of problems. Level A, technical: can the symbols be sent accurately? Level B, semantic: do the symbols carry the intended meaning? Level C, effectiveness: does the meaning change behaviour as intended?
Shannon developed it while working on telephone systems, so it fits a network built from telephone lines and radios.

## Slide 18: Comparing the two theories

[Speaker 5, about 1.25 minutes]
Let's compare the two theories.
The 7 Cs check the quality of one message, and anyone can use them quickly. Shannon and Weaver's model follows a message through a network and shows where it can be lost or distorted.
Weakness: the 7 Cs are a checklist rather than a tested theory. Shannon and Weaver's model is one-way. It has no feedback, and it says nothing about the relationship between people. Craig notes that this "transmission" view of communication is often criticised as too simple.
Culture: both theories assume that meaning sits in the explicit message. Hall called this low-context communication. Hong Kong is often described as more high-context than Britain. In high-context cultures, much of the meaning depends on the relationship and the situation. One caution: Cardon's 2008 review found that Hall's model has rarely been tested. So the 7 Cs help us check one order, and Shannon and Weaver help us check the whole network. Part 3 uses both.

## Slide 19: Part 3 divider

[Speaker 5, about 10 seconds]
Part 3. How do the theories explain the network, and what do they miss?

## Slide 20: The network, part by part

[Speaker 5, about 1.25 minutes]
Let's put the network on Shannon and Weaver's model.
Read the top row against the bottom row. The source is the radar stations and observers. The transmitter is the Filter Room and the plotters. The noise is duplicate, conflicting and old plots. The destination is the controllers and the pilots.
Now look at the dashed line. The network also had feedback: radio stations fixed the position of the RAF's own fighters, and pilots reported what they saw. The original model has no feedback, so here the network went further than the theory.
The network reduced all three of Weaver's problems. Level A: the Filter Room removed duplicate and conflicting plots before they reached the tables. Level B: shared symbols and code words carried the same meaning to everyone. Level C: squadrons met the raids. But it did not remove the problems. Radar plots were not always accurate, and on the next slide two commanders disagree about what to do with the same reports.

## Slide 21: One radio order against the 7 Cs

[Speaker 6, about 1.25 minutes]
Now the 7 Cs. We built an example order from the code words in Dowding's despatch: "Scramble. Vector 230. Angels 20." Our group scored it from one to three dots. It is clear, correct, five words long, exact, complete, and written for a pilot who is flying.
Courtesy scores only one dot. There is no "please". In combat, speed came first.
So, by the checklist, the order scores 19 of 21. We gave courtesy one dot to show a weakness of the 7 Cs. In an office, an order with no "please" sounds rude. In a cockpit, a short order respects the pilot's time. The checklist does not ask about the situation, so the same five words could score one dot or three.

## Slide 22: Three limits of our analysis

[Speaker 6, about 1.25 minutes]
Our analysis has three limits.
First, same network, different tactics. Keith Park and Trafford Leigh-Mallory, two of Dowding's group commanders, received reports from the same network and still disagreed over how to use their squadrons. Shannon and Weaver explain how information moved, but not how people agreed on what to do with it.
Second, many people built the network. It built on air defence from the First World War and on scientists' radar work, and thousands of plotters and observers ran it every day.
Third, most of our sources come from British or RAF institutions, and Galland wrote after the war.
Here's a question for you: if thousands of people ran the network, how much of its success belongs to Dowding?

## Slide 23: Part 4 divider

[Speaker 6, about 10 seconds]
Part 4. How can a student team get the right information to the right person in time?

## Slide 24: Our group chats have the same problem

[Speaker 6, about 1 minute]
Our group projects have the same problem as the radar operator.
Amy posts in a five-person group chat: "Can someone do the theory part?" What happens next?
Option A: nobody replies. Each member thinks someone else will do it, and on Thursday the theory part is still empty.
Option B: two members both start. They write the same section twice, and nobody starts the comparison table.
Like the radar operator in 1940, Amy has the information. The hard part is getting it to the right person, in a form that person can act on. Dowding's network gives us two lessons.

## Slide 25: Lesson 1: Keep one shared tracker

[Speaker 6, about 1.5 minutes]
Lesson 1: keep one shared tracker, and run it through the same four steps as Dowding's network.
Report: radar and observers reported to one network, so everyone posts updates in one shared tracker, not in side chats.
Filter: the Filter Room removed duplicate plots, so one coordinator merges duplicate or conflicting updates twice a week.
Display: the tote board used fixed words, so the tracker uses four status words: not started, drafting, ready for review, done.
Order: a controller said "Vector 230, Angels 20", so every request names an owner, a task and a deadline: "Ming: theory slides, Thursday 8 pm."
Look at the example tracker. Everyone can see that the comparison table has an owner, Jason, but has not started. Amy's problem from the last slide cannot happen.
Research supports the goal. In a flight simulation study, Mathieu and colleagues found that pairs who shared an understanding of the task and of each other worked together better and performed better. The study did not test trackers. The tracker is our way to build that shared understanding in a student team.
One tip for students: rotate the coordinator each week, so one person does not do all the filtering.

## Slide 26: Lesson 2: Confirm what you heard

[Speaker 6, about 1.25 minutes]
Lesson 2: confirm what you heard.
Shannon and Weaver's model has no feedback, but Dowding's network did, through radio position fixes and pilots' reports. A team can add feedback in four steps, adapted from the active listening work of Carl Rogers and Richard Farson.
Listen to the whole request. Paraphrase it in your own words. Check: "Did I get that right?" And start once the other person confirms.
On the right is Amy's chat again. Ming's paraphrase makes Amy remember the comparison table, which Ming would otherwise have left out.
In a 2014 study of 115 people, Weger and colleagues found that speakers felt more understood when the listener paraphrased and asked follow-up questions than when the listener gave advice or only short replies. The study measured how understood people felt, not fewer mistakes, so our chat is an example, not proof.
Use this for every request with a deadline. A paraphrase checks the request without saying the other person was unclear.

## Slide 27: Conclusion and Q&A

[Speaker 1, about 45 seconds, then Q&A]
Let's go back to our first question: if you commanded Britain's fighters, where would you send them? Dowding's answer was: only where the operations table showed a raid was going. On 15 September, Park had no squadron left in reserve, yet the Germans lost about 56 to 60 aircraft. Germany had more aircraft. Britain had a network that got the right information to the right person in time, and that is our definition of effective communication.
For our own teams, three habits: send every update to one shared tracker, write each request as owner, task and deadline, and confirm what you heard before you act.
Two questions for you. Where does information get lost in your group projects? And who in your team merges the updates, the way the Filter Room did?
Thank you. We're happy to take your questions.

Prepared answers for likely questions:
- "Did Britain win only because of the network?" No. The pilots' skill, aircraft production and German mistakes, such as the switch to London, also mattered. The network made the best use of a limited force.
- "Was the network Dowding's idea alone?" No. It built on First World War air defence and on radar science. Dowding's contribution was to organise these parts into one network and to insist that information was filtered and shared.
- "Why Shannon and Weaver, not a newer model?" Their model describes a message moving through a technical system, which is what the network was. Its missing feedback loop is also our Lesson 2.
