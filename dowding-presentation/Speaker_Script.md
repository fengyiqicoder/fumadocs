# Speaker script: The battle won by information

About 22 minutes at 130 words per minute, plus Q&A. Speaker numbers are suggestions, so reassign them to your group.

## Slide 1: Front page

[Speaker 1, about 20 seconds]
Good morning. Our leadership quality is effective communication. Our story is about a battle that was decided as much by information as by aircraft, and about the man who built the network that carried that information: Air Chief Marshal Hugh Dowding.

## Slide 2: Britain stands alone

[Speaker 1, about 1 minute]
Imagine it is July 1940, and you command Britain's fighters. Germany has defeated France in six weeks, and on 22 June France signed an armistice. The German army stands on the French coast, and Britain is almost alone.
But Germany cannot simply sail across the Channel. Before any invasion, its air force must destroy the Royal Air Force. On 10 July, the attack begins. You can see the defenders here: Spitfires of No. 610 Squadron, photographed two weeks later.
Now, a question for you. German raids can come from anywhere along the coast. Where would you send these fighters?
Keep your answer in mind. Dowding faced exactly this question.

## Slide 3: Part 1 divider

[Speaker 1, about 10 seconds]
Part 1, the story. One question runs through it: how did Britain meet every raid in time, with no fighters to spare?

## Slide 4: 750 British fighters, 2,250 German aircraft

[Speaker 1, about 50 seconds]
First, the enemy was strong. The Luftwaffe had just helped defeat Poland, Norway and France.
Look at the chart. On 10 August 1940, Germany had more than 1,300 bombers and more than 950 fighters ready to fly. Britain had 1,100 fighters ready to fly, but trained pilots for only about 750 of them.
So Britain could not win by having more aircraft. Its 750 fighters had to find and stop more than 1,300 bombers, while German fighters protected them.
Every squadron had to be in the right place. A squadron sent to the wrong place could not reach the real raid in time.

## Slide 5: Twenty minutes to meet each raid

[Speaker 1, about 50 seconds]
And there was very little time.
A radar station in Kent could detect a raid forming over France about 20 minutes before it reached London. 11 Group, which defended London and the south-east, warned its controllers that raiders could be over London 20 minutes after radar first detected them.
Out of those 20 minutes, the network needed about 4 to put the raid on the operations room table. At times, the controller, the officer who sent the fighters, had about 2 minutes to decide where to send them.
So every minute spent passing a report along was a minute less for the fighters to reach the raid.

## Slide 6: Seeing the raid was not enough

[Speaker 2, about 1 minute]
Let's try a thought experiment. You are a radar operator. On your screen, 100 aircraft are crossing the Channel. What do you do?
Option A: you phone the nearest airfield. A few fighters take off on their own. Nobody else knows where the raid is going, and the other airfields sit and wait.
Option B: you report to headquarters. Each level studies the report and passes an order down. By the time the order reaches the pilots, the bombs have already fallen.
Both options fail. Seeing the raid was only the start. The hard part was getting the right report to the right person in time.

## Slide 7: Our group chats have the same problem

[Speaker 2, about 50 seconds]
And you have seen this problem yourself.
Amy posts in a five-person group chat: "Can someone do the theory part?" What happens next?
Option A: nobody replies. Each member thinks someone else will do it, and on Thursday the theory part is still empty.
Option B: two members both start. They write the same section twice, so one person's work is wasted, and nobody starts the comparison table.
Like the radar operator, Amy has the information. The hard part is getting it to the right person, in a form that person can act on.
In 1940, Britain solved this problem. Let's see how, and keep Amy in mind as we go.

## Slide 8: The man who built the network

[Speaker 2, about 1 minute]
The man who solved it was Hugh Dowding. In this photo from June 1940, he is the man in the centre with his hands behind his back, while the King decorates one of his pilots.
Dowding had one clear idea. In our words: send each squadron to a raid that someone has already reported, instead of sending it to search the sky. A squadron that searched empty sky found no raid, and Britain had no spare squadrons.
In 1935, as the RAF's head of research, he backed radar after a successful test near Daventry. In 1936, he became the first head of Fighter Command. Over the next four years, he linked radar stations, observers, operations rooms and pilots into one network.
Let's follow a raid through that network, in four steps. Each step solves a problem left by the one before.

## Slide 9: Step 1: Report

[Speaker 2, about 1 minute]
Step 1: report.
On the left is a Chain Home radar station. About 20 of them stood along the coast. But radar had a weakness: the stations faced out to sea. Once a raid crossed the coast, radar could not track it.
So Dowding used people. In the middle is the Observer Corps: about 30,000 volunteers at about 1,000 posts. When a raid crossed the coast, they spotted it by eye and reported it by telephone.
Every report went into one network.
But the radar plots had a problem of their own. Several plots often showed the same raid, and some gave a different position or size for it.

## Slide 10: Step 2: Filter

[Speaker 2, about 1 minute]
Step 2: filter.
Radar reports went first to the Filter Room at Fighter Command headquarters. There, staff compared the plots, removed the duplicates and kept one track, the raid's checked position and direction, for each raid.
The radar operator's Option B lacked this step: one report sent to every level at once. In Dowding's own account, each filtered track went by telephone to the Command, Group and Sector tables at the same time. Every level saw the same raid at the same moment, and nobody waited for an order to be passed down.
But now commanders at three levels had to read their tables quickly, and they had to know how old each plot was.

## Slide 11: Step 3: Display

[Speaker 3, about 1 minute]
Step 3: display.
This photo shows the operations room at Fighter Command headquarters, with plotters around the map table and officers above. Every table in the network used the same symbols.
The sector clock had red, yellow and blue segments, each 5 minutes long. Each marker took the colour of the clock segment in which it was placed, so a controller could see at once how old each plot was. A raid block showed the raid's name and its estimated strength. The tote board showed the state of each squadron, such as "at readiness" or "left ground".
One problem remained: the pilot. A pilot in a cockpit needed his orders in a few words.

## Slide 12: Step 4: Order

[Speaker 3, about 1 minute]
Step 4: order.
Group headquarters decided which sector sent fighters to each raid, and how many. Then the sector controller spoke to the pilots by radio, in the code words Dowding lists in his despatch.
"Scramble" meant take off. "Vector 230" meant fly a course of 230 degrees. "Angels 20" meant fly at 20,000 feet. "Pancake" meant land. A whole order took only a few words, and it went to one squadron.
The photo shows pilots of No. 19 Squadron running to their aircraft. It was staged for the camera, but a real scramble looked much like this.
So: report, filter, display, order. Did it work?

## Slide 13: We had nothing like it

[Speaker 3, about 45 seconds]
Let's hear what the enemy said.
Adolf Galland was a German fighter commander in 1940. He later wrote that the British had "an extraordinary advantage", which was "their radar and fighter control network and organisation". And then: "We had nothing like it."
On 15 September, that advantage helped Britain beat off two large German attacks.

## Slide 14: Almost every squadron found its raid

[Speaker 4, about 1 minute]
15 September 1940. The Luftwaffe sent two large daylight attacks against London. That day, Fighter Command scrambled 51 squadrons, and according to the historian Alfred Price, 50 of them made contact with the raiders. Almost no squadron was sent to empty sky.
Both attacks were beaten off. Germany lost 56 aircraft. Britain lost 28, and 12 RAF pilots were killed.
Two days later, on 17 September, Hitler postponed the invasion of Britain until further notice.
The photo shows the operations room at Uxbridge, preserved today. Churchill watched this table that day. He later wrote: "I now asked, 'What other reserves have we?' 'There are none,' said Air Vice Marshal Park." Every squadron was in the air, and the network sent almost every one of them to a raid.
So how did a smaller force win?

## Slide 15: Britain did not have more. It wasted less.

[Speaker 4, about 1.25 minutes]
Britain could not get more fighters, so it won by wasting fewer of them. Almost no squadron was sent to empty sky, because the right information reached the right person in time. That is our key idea: a team that cannot get more can still waste less, by getting the right information to the right person in time. Amy's team is in the same position: it has five members and one deadline, and it cannot get more members or more time.
That gives us our definition: effective communication means the right information reaches the right person in time, in a form they can act on. Let's check the network against each part.
Right information: the Filter Room kept one track per raid. Right person: each track reached the Command, Group and Sector tables, and Group chose which sector met the raid. In time: a raid appeared on the table about 4 minutes after radar detected it. In a form they can act on: "Vector 230, Angels 20".
We built this definition from two scholars. Keyton, as cited in Lunenburg, says communication transmits "information and common understanding from one person to another". Spitzberg says communication is competent when the other person judges it effective and appropriate. The two agree that a message must reach the other person. They differ in focus. Keyton looks at what is transmitted, which gives us the right information reaching the right person. Spitzberg looks at how the receiver judges it, which gives us a form they can act on. Neither mentions time, so we added it from the story.

## Slide 16: Part 2 divider

[Speaker 4, about 15 seconds]
Part 2. The network sent almost no squadron to empty sky. In Amy's chat, one section stays empty and another is written twice. Two theories of communication explain why the network wasted so little and Amy's chat wastes so much.

## Slide 17: Theory 1: Shannon and Weaver

[Speaker 5, about 1.25 minutes]
Our first theory comes from engineering. In 1949, Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver described communication as a chain. An information source creates a message. A transmitter turns it into a signal. The signal travels through a channel. A receiver turns it back into a message, and the message reaches its destination. On the way, noise can distort it.
Let's put Amy's chat on the chain. Amy is the source. Her request travels through the group chat. The noise is the dozens of other messages around it. Five phones receive it, but the destination, the member who should act, is not named.
Weaver described three levels of problems, and Amy's chat has all three. Technical: her message arrives, but under dozens of others. Semantic: "the theory part" means different things to different members. Effectiveness: nobody starts the work.

## Slide 18: Theory 2: The 7 Cs

[Speaker 5, about 1 minute]
Shannon and Weaver's model follows the path of a message. Our second theory is about the message itself.
Murphy, Hildebrandt and Thomas list seven qualities of a good message, the 7 Cs. We phrase each as a question. Clarity: what is the one main point? Correctness: are the facts and tone right? Conciseness: can it be said in fewer words? Concreteness: is it backed by specific facts? Completeness: does the reader have all they need to act? Consideration: is it written from the reader's side? Courtesy: is it respectful, even when firm?
So we use Shannon and Weaver to check the path of a message, and the 7 Cs to check the message itself. Now let's test the network, and Amy, against both.

## Slide 19: Part 3 divider

[Speaker 5, about 10 seconds]
Part 3. Does Dowding's network pass both theories, and does Amy's chat?

## Slide 20: The Filter Room removed the noise first

[Speaker 5, about 1.25 minutes]
First, the path. Each part of Shannon and Weaver's chain matches a part of the network. The source is the radar stations and observers. The transmitter is the Filter Room and the plotters, who turned raw reports into tracks and markers. The channel is telephone lines and radio. The destination is the controllers and the pilots.
Look at the noise box: duplicate, conflicting and old plots. The network removed that noise before anyone read the table, so controllers did not waste their few minutes on duplicate plots. Amy's message also reaches everyone, but unfiltered, and nobody knows who should act.
The dashed line shows something the model leaves out. The network had feedback: radio stations worked out the position of the RAF's own fighters, and pilots reported what they saw. Shannon and Weaver's original model is one-way, so here the network went further than the theory.

## Slide 21: The radio order passes; Amy's request fails

[Speaker 6, about 1.25 minutes]
Now the message. We compared a radio order built from Dowding's code words, "Scramble. Vector 230. Angels 20.", with Amy's "Can someone do the theory part?" We scored each from one to three dots. These scores are our group's judgment.
The order names one squadron, one course and one height. Amy names no one, gives no deadline, and leaves the reader to guess. So the order scores 19 and Amy scores 13. For every C that Amy misses, her teammates must guess, and a wrong guess wastes their work.
But look at courtesy. The order has no "please", and Amy's request is polite. In a cockpit, speed mattered more than courtesy. In a group chat, courtesy matters. In the 7 Cs, every quality has the same weight in every situation, but the right weight depends on the situation. This equal weighting is a real weakness of the theory, and it is why our second practice adds a "please".

## Slide 22: Part 4 divider

[Speaker 6, about 15 seconds]
Part 4. The network passed both theories, and Amy's chat failed both. Amy's team cannot get more members or more time, so, like Britain, it has to waste less. The good news: Amy's team already has the tool it needs. Look back at Fighter Command's operations table.

## Slide 23: Look familiar? It is a task board

[Speaker 6, about 1 minute]
Does it look familiar? It should. On the left is a real Trello board, Trello's own project management template. Many of you use a board like this already, in Trello, Notion or Microsoft Planner.
Fighter Command's operations table worked the same way. One table served every level, just as one board serves the whole team. A raid block became a card. The tote board's few fixed words became lists: To Do, Pending and Done. The 20-minute limit became a due date on the card. And an order to one squadron became a card assigned to one member.
In 1940, Dowding built a system that worked like the boards we now use every week. The question is whether we use it the way Fighter Command did. Two practices show how.

## Slide 24: Practice 1: One task board for the project

[Speaker 6, about 1.25 minutes]
Practice 1: run the project on one task board, set up like the operations table.
One board for the whole project, where every task is a card. If a task is not on the board, nobody will work on it, just as a raid that was not on the table did not get a squadron.
One owner and one due date per card, like the raid block's name and strength: "Theory slides, Ming, Thursday."
Four lists, Not started, Drafting, Ready for review and Done, like the tote board's few fixed words.
And a roadmap of deadlines. Fighter Command lived with a 20-minute limit. A roadmap shows the team, at a glance, how much time is left before each deadline. On the right is Amy's: research and story slides are done, and the theory slides are due on Thursday.
Research supports this practice. In a flight simulation study, Mathieu and colleagues found that pairs who shared an understanding of the task and of each other worked together better and performed better. A board does not guarantee a shared understanding, but it shows the whole team the same tasks, owners and deadlines. Free tools such as Trello, Notion or Microsoft Planner are enough.

## Slide 25: Practice 2: Pass updates through the board

[Speaker 6, about 1.25 minutes]
Practice 2: pass updates through the board, following the network's path.
Report once: update the card, and do not repeat it in the group chat. In 1940, every report went into one network.
Filter in one place: twice a week, one member, the coordinator, merges duplicate cards and closes old ones, as the Filter Room did.
Assign, do not ask: mention one person on the card, "@Ming, theory slides by Thursday 8 pm, please." That is a controller's order to one squadron.
Confirm: the owner replies "Got it" or moves the card to Drafting, just as pilots reported back by radio. That is the feedback Shannon and Weaver's model lacks.
On the right is the difference. In the group chat, five people read Amy's request and nobody acts. On the board, one person reads it and that person acts.
Two studies support simple requests and confirmation. Bhargava and Manoli found that simpler letters led more of 35,050 tax filers to claim a benefit they were eligible for. Weger and colleagues found that speakers felt more understood when the listener paraphrased.
One caution for Hong Kong. Hall describes cultures like Hong Kong's as high-context: much of a request is left unsaid, which may be why "Can someone" feels safer than naming a person. A card that names one person can feel too direct, so add a "please". Cardon's review found that Hall's model has rarely been tested, so we treat this as a caution, not a rule.

## Slide 26: Conclusion and Q&A

[Speaker 1, about 45 seconds, then Q&A]
Let's go back to our first question: if you commanded Britain's fighters, where would you send them? Dowding's answer was: only where the operations table showed a raid was going.
Germany had more aircraft. Britain wasted less. Your team, like Amy's, cannot get more members or more time. But it can waste less by getting the right information to the right person in time. Amy's request now sits on a card assigned to Ming, Ming moves it to Drafting, and the theory part is ready by Thursday.
Our two practices, as three habits: one board, read by everyone. Update the card, not the chat. And assign each task to one person.
Two questions for you. Where does information get lost in your group projects? And does your team have a board, or only a group chat?
Thank you. We're happy to take your questions.

Prepared answers for likely questions:
- "Did Britain win only because of the network?" No. The pilots' skill, aircraft production and German mistakes also mattered. The network made the best use of a limited force.
- "Was the network Dowding's idea alone?" No. It built on First World War air defence and on radar science, and thousands of plotters and observers ran it. Dowding's contribution was to organise these parts into one network. Most of our sources are British or RAF institutions, and Galland wrote after the war, so we may be giving the network more credit than a German historian would.
- "Is a task board not just more work?" Updating a card takes less time than reading a long group chat to find out who is doing what. The network spent minutes on filtering so that controllers did not waste them on duplicate plots.
- "Why Shannon and Weaver, not a newer model?" Their model describes a message moving through a technical system, which is what the network was. Its missing feedback loop is why our second practice ends with a confirmation.
